Unlike classification metrics that deal with "right or wrong" categories, regression metrics deal with distances. They measure how close your model's numerical predictions are to the actual, true values.
Let's look at a real-world scenario to see how these metrics work and when to use them.

## The Scenario: Predicting House Prices
Imagine you built a machine learning model to estimate home values for a real estate website. We test the model on 3 different houses:

| House | Actual Price | Model's Predicted Price | The Error (Actual - Predicted) |
|---|---|---|---|
| House 1 | ₹50,00,000 | ₹52,00,000 | -₹2,00,000 (Overestimated) |
| House 2 | ₹80,00,000 | ₹79,00,000 | +₹1,00,000 (Underestimated) |
| House 3 | ₹1,20,00,000 | ₹1,50,00,000 | -₹30,00,000 (Massive Overestimate) |

Notice House 3: The model made a huge mistake here, missing the price by a whopping ₹30 Lakhs (an outlier error).

## 1. MAE (Mean Absolute Error)
MAE calculates the average size of the mistakes, completely ignoring whether they were overestimates or underestimates. It treats all errors linearly.

* How it works: It takes the absolute value of each error (making them all positive) and averages them.
* Calculation:
$$\text{MAE} = \frac{2,00,00,0 + 1,00,00,0 + 30,00,00,0}{3} = \frac{33,00,00,0}{3} = \mathbf{₹11,00,00,0}$$ 
* The Verdict: On average, your model misses the true house price by ₹11 Lakhs.
* Best used when: You want an easy-to-understand error metric in your exact original units, and you don't want occasional massive errors (like House 3) to completely distort the final score.

## 2. MSE (Mean Squared Error)
MSE squares the errors before averaging them.

* How it works: Squaring the numbers removes negative signs, but it does something else critical: it exponentially punishes large errors.
* Calculation:
$$\text{MSE} = \frac{(2,00,00,0)^2 + (1,00,00,0)^2 + (30,00,00,0)^2}{3} = \frac{4 \times 10^{10} + 1 \times 10^{10} + 900 \times 10^{10}}{3} = \mathbf{301.6 \times 10^{10}}$$ 
* The Verdict: The result is a massive, abstract number that is hard for humans to visualize.
* Best used when: You are actively training a model. Mathematical optimization algorithms prefer MSE because it creates a smooth curve to minimize.

## 3. RMSE (Root Mean Squared Error)
RMSE fixes the visualization problem of MSE by taking its square root, bringing the final number back into the original currency/units.

* Calculation:
$$\text{RMSE} = \sqrt{301.6 \times 10^{10}} \approx \mathbf{₹17,36,85,5}$$ 
* Why is it different from MAE? Notice that while the MAE was ₹11 Lakhs, the RMSE shot up to ₹17.3 Lakhs. Why? Because House 3's massive error was squared, heavily dragging the entire metric up.
* Best used when: Large errors are completely unacceptable. If missing a prediction by a mile ruins your business or system, RMSE is the metric to look at because it highlights those big failures.

## 4. R-Squared ($R^2$ / Coefficient of Determination)
While MAE and RMSE tell you the absolute error amount, $R^2$ tells you the relative performance on a scale from 0 to 1. It asks: How much better is my model than a dumb model that just guesses the overall average house price for every home?

* How to read the score:
* 0.0: Your model is completely useless; it's no better than guessing the baseline average.
   * 1.0: A perfect model with zero errors.
* The Example: If your house model gets an $R^2$ of 0.85, it means your model’s features (square footage, location, bedrooms) successfully explain 85% of the variances in home prices. The remaining 15% is random noise or missing information.

## Direct Regression Metric Comparison

| Metric | Interpretation | Unit | How to React to It |
|---|---|---|---|
| MAE | Average raw mistake size. | Same as target (₹) | Highly robust. Treats small errors and huge errors equally. |
| RMSE | Average mistake size, penalising outliers. | Same as target (₹) | Sensitive to outliers. Use this if you want to eliminate massive misses. |
| $R^2$ | Goodness of fit variance score. | Percentage / Ratio (0 to 1) | Excellent for comparing models across entirely different datasets. |
